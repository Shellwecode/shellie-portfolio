## Overview

Cortex is Five9's new enterprise AI voice agent builder — a platform where
agentic and deterministic agent systems are both first-class. It's ongoing
work, currently in user validation and executive alignment.

I shipped the first version of Cortex on my own. For 2.0, I partnered with our
design lead and ran the research that shaped and validated the new information
architecture and interaction patterns the platform is built on. Along the way
we built design tooling to scale the practice to the whole org — *2 product
designers, 3 PMs, 50+ engineers*.

> *Design a self-service platform for AI agent builders, where they can choose
> between autonomous and rule-based behavior, and be given precise control over
> agent decisions at enterprise scale.*

## The problem

Enterprises wanted agentic AI, but every existing solution forced a choice.
Fully agentic systems couldn't meet enterprise standards for trust, governance,
and compliance; fully deterministic systems couldn't adapt to calls that don't
follow a script. Combining the two meant fragmentation and risk. Five9 needed a
platform where both paradigms were first-class, not a compromise.

The timeline for 1.0 was tight — I was the only designer when we started. It
worked, but it was the version built around the deadline, not the one I'd have
built with time to think.

Two problems surfaced after launch:

1. **The terminology was a wall.** Prompt, operating procedure, instruction —
   three words for what users experienced as the same thing. They pointed to
   slightly different technical concepts, but users couldn't tell them apart.
2. **The mental model didn't form.** 1.0 never communicated the multi-agent
   hierarchy early or clearly enough. Users understood the idea but couldn't
   internalize it — the product complicated the system instead of explaining it.

## Research

I spent the weeks after launch studying what we had shipped and where the gaps
were. I interviewed our forward-deployed engineers who author agent prompts for
customers, and reached out to voice agent builders on online forums to
understand their mental models and how they get a voice agent into production.

I also mapped the market. Most platforms still relied on a single text field
for agent behavior; others leaned into pure flow-based automation. Almost no
one had built a seamless hybrid of the two — resolving this tension was a
category bet, and it confirmed the 1.0 findings were valid.

Two patterns kept showing up in how builders actually work: they start from
prebuilt templates rather than blank prompts, and they iterate with AI — using
it to draft, revise, and stress-test their work. When Owen joined as design
lead and we started on 2.0, these findings became the brief.

## Explorations

The thesis from 1.0 was clear: builders shouldn't have to author rules,
instructions, and behavior in the same place. The design problem was turning
one big prompt field into a system of scoped, structured configuration without
losing the flexibility builders rely on.

We developed a lifecycle-based IA — configuration mapped to the stages of
building, deploying, and operating an agent. *Configure* holds everything that
defines an agent; *Test* holds everything for testing and debugging. Builders
move between stages without losing their place in the work.

The bigger move was in the language itself. In 1.0, an agent was an
orchestrator over subagents, each holding prompts, tools, variables, and
knowledge — too many behaviors happening at once to tell where one piece of
work ended and another began. In 2.0 we split the model into *Agent* and
*Task*, and did the same for prompts, guardrails, and transfers. A guardrail
that applies to every task lives at the agent level; a tool only one task needs
lives on that task. The hierarchy 1.0 hid in language is the hierarchy 2.0
makes visible.

## Final design

Templates are the first surface, and AI is built into the authoring experience.
A builder can ask it to draft a task description, tighten a guardrail, or
rewrite an instruction without leaving the surface. The AI behaves differently
at different levels — at the agent level it generates tasks and guardrails; at
the task level it drafts and refines prompts and suggests next steps.

The editor lets builders compose with the same primitives the IA defines —
tools, variables, knowledge dropped inline — and power users move through them
with keyboard shortcuts.

Testing runs alongside authoring. A builder can start a test call while
configuring a task to see where it holds and where it breaks. The
build–test–rewrite loop collapses into a single flow: iterate on one section of
a task prompt, test it in isolation, and trace each outcome back to the
configuration choice that caused it.

## Impact

Cortex 2.0's spine — the lifecycle IA, the agent/task split, AI-in-context
authoring — is the architecture the platform is now being validated against,
with users and with executives. The research and patterns from this work also
became the brief for org-wide design tooling, scaling the practice beyond one
project.

What I'm still pondering:

- **Where should procedural work end and agentic capability begin?** The hybrid
  model presumes you can tell automation and agentic apart — but the two blur
  within a single flow.
- **What's the right home for cross-agent context?** The agent/task split
  clarified scope at two levels, but real systems have shared knowledge, shared
  guardrails, shared behavior.
- **How do we keep the builder's intent legible when AI is in the loop?** As
  builders iterate with AI, configuration drifts from what they would have
  written alone. When does that become a problem?
