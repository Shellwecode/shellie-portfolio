## 张晓风

I was eight when I first read 张晓风. 《我在》 was the one that opened my
world. There was a period of time I was obsessed with writing the character
**在** (*zài*). It means being here and present. Reading 《我在》, the piece
built on a single word, I saw the way her sentences moved and connected the
small dignity of a child's presence to the bigger world around her.

I read her as 散文家, or an essayist. The school curriculum had filed her
there. So had every anthology I'd ever pulled off a shelf.

Today I learned she also wrote 《潘渡娜》 in 1968. *Pandora*, in the
Taiwanese transliteration; the mainland would later render it 潘多拉. The
story, published in 中国时报 on September 12 of that year, is now recognized
in Taiwanese SF circles as the first piece of Chinese-language science
fiction. 1968. A year before Le Guin's *The Left Hand of Darkness*. Years
before almost anything English-language readers think of as the start of
feminist SF.

She wrote it in a room that hadn't been built. The terms 科幻小说 and
科幻文学 didn't crystallize as stable categories in Taiwan until around
1980. When 《潘渡娜》 appeared, "Taiwanese SF" was not yet a thing the field
could see. Two other writers — 张系国 and 黄海 — published their first SF
stories that same year. Both later became 鼻祖 of Taiwan SF: founding
figures, biographies built around the fact. The third writer was filed as
散文家 and stayed there.

So which is it: was the genre invisible to her, or was she invisible to the
genre?

## 夏笳 and Ken Liu

During my first year of college, I took a writing class on Weird Fiction. I
read Xia Jia's 《百鬼夜行街》, *A Hundred Ghosts Parade Tonight*, first in
Ken Liu's translation. It was the first time I'd read a Chinese writer in
English before reading her in Chinese.

At the time, Ken Liu's name felt more visible because he had translated the
well-known Three Body Problem series into English. I learned about Xia Jia
and her stories through him. This was an inverted reading experience: I met
the translator before I met the author, even though all three of us move
between Chinese and English.

Reading 《百鬼夜行街》 in English made me ponder the human-robot spectrum
and the meaning of being human. But the Chinese version, with its extremely
poetic language, added another register that reads more like fantasy.
"假作真时真亦假" pulls me into the dreamy world 《红楼梦》 fabricates.

Looking back, how much of what I thought 《百鬼夜行街》 was about came from
Xia Jia, and how much from Ken Liu's hand?

## Recanonization

After my Weird Fiction semester, I read an interview where Xia Jia called
her own work "porridge SF." Not hard SF. Something she had to invent a name
for.

Little me, reading in Chinese first, would have called 《百鬼夜行街》 玄幻
(fantasy), not 科幻 (SF). The genre line had already been drawn before I
knew to look for it. A man writing speculative fiction at scale gets read as
科幻. A woman writing the same kind of speculative fiction at scale gets
read as 玄幻 or 仙侠, and excluded from the SF canon.

科幻 carries the weight of conquered space and won wars. The rest goes
elsewhere. So who draws the genre line? And who gets filed by it?
